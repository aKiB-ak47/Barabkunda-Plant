Cylinder calibration: are we giving gas away?

Fill accuracy, spread, filling-head bias, over-delivery and a machine-learning forecast of the next check, from the plant’s own weight-calibration records

Prepared by: Akib Fateh, Barabkunda LPG Plant (CTG)

Date: 28 October 2026

A decision-support review of cylinder fill accuracy. Every figure was computed from 972 valid 12 kg check-weighings across 41 rounds (Jun 2024 to 25 Oct 2026), plus 124 larger-cylinder checks. Safety findings come first.

Safety items — for review by a qualified engineer

S1 · Systematic over-filling

12 kg cylinders averaged +47 g over nominal in 2026; 9 checks were more than 300 g over and 3 more than 500 g over (worst +600 g). Over-filling reduces the vapour space a cylinder needs for safety.

S2 · Larger cylinders over-filled together

On 6 Jan 2026, head 3 recorded +800 g on 20, 35 and 45 kg cylinders alike — a pattern that points to one mis-set head or a transcription error. Verify against the original record.

S3 · No reject step recorded

Out-of-tolerance cylinders appear in the after-filling spot samples (10.4% over 150 g), but the sheets record no re-work or rejection of them (ISO 10691 expects checks before, during and after filling).

Filling ratio cannot be verified from these records: cylinder water capacity and tare stamp are not in the data. Decision support only.

References: NFPA 58 container filling limits; ISO 10691; Bangladesh LPG Rules 2004 (Department of Explosives).

2

These three findings are safety-relevant and must be confirmed by a qualified engineer against the physical cylinders and the filling-ratio rules. Over-filling is the headline risk; the +800 g larger-cylinder record and the absence of a reject step need checking against original documents and procedures.

Executive summary

  1. The line over-fills. Across 972 checks the average 12 kg cylinder held +43 g too much gas (95% CI 36–49 g). In 2026 the bias rose to +47 g and the latest check (25 Oct) averaged +95 g.
  2. Consistency has halved. The spread of fills widened from 49 g in Jun 2024 to 118 g in 2026. Capability Cpk against a ±150 g tolerance fell from 0.85 to 0.29 — far below the 1.33 benchmark.
  3. A few heads drive most of it. 5 of 24 filling heads over-fill significantly; the worst (heads 5, 14, 13, 6) run +70 to +100 g. Recalibrating those would recover most of the bias.
  4. The giveaway is material. Interpolated over 2026, over-filling gave away about 42.5 MT of LPG (95% range 12–73 MT) — roughly 178 kg a day.
  5. Without action the next check fails too. An LSTM neural net forecasts the next round at +79 g average, with about 8 of 24 heads outside ±150 g (backtest MAPE 0.62% on net content).

3

Five points a manager should leave with: the line over-fills, consistency has worsened, a handful of heads cause most of it, the lost gas is material, and the problem is predicted to persist without recalibration.

Executive Summary

  1. The filling line overfills 12 kg cylinders by 42.7 g on average (95% CI 36–49 g). This comes from 972 valid check-weighs on 37 days. In 2026 the average is 47.4 g; 55% of cylinders were over and 18% under.
  2. Precision has fallen sharply since 2024. The spread more than doubled, from 49 g to 118 g, and capability (Cpk against ±150 g) dropped from 0.85 to 0.29, against a usual target of 1.33. Only 1 of 32 check rounds in 2026 stayed inside the 2024 control limits. The step change came on 10 January 2026.
  3. Most of the error is random scatter from cylinder to cylinder, not a wrong setting. Scatter is 84% of the variation, day-to-day drift 10% and differences between heads 6%. The machines correctly allow for each cylinder's tare and leftover gas. The fix is therefore re-zeroing the set-points plus maintenance of fill valves and load cells. 17 of 24 heads overfill significantly; heads 5, 14, 13, 6 and 21 are the worst, at +70 to +100 g.
  4. About 42.5 MT of LPG was over-delivered from 1 March to 25 October 2026 (95% CI 11.8–73.2 MT). This weights each day's bias by the 2.05 million 12 kg cylinders actually filled. It replaces the rough ~99 MT I gave earlier in chat, which assumed a flat bias. Over-filling also means the tank stock should fall faster than the books show, which makes the stock gain in the operations report even harder to explain.
  5. The next check is forecast to average +79 g (80% range +77 to +121 g), with about 8 of 24 heads outside ±150 g. The forecast comes from an LSTM neural network, the most accurate of 7 methods tested: backtest RMSE 97.8 g and MAPE 0.62% of net content, 14.5% better than a naive forecast. Heads 5, 3, 21, 11 and 14 are the most likely to fail.

Analysis

1. Control charts: out of control since January 2026

The 2024 checks set the baseline: an average round error of +26 g, a spread of 48 g, and control limits of −4 g to +56 g for the round average. In 2026, 24 of 32 rounds broke the average limit and 29 of 32 broke the spread limit. Only one round was within both.

A change-point model finds no lasting shift in the average error, but a clear step in spread on 10 January 2026, from 56 g to 109 g. The 10 January round was the worst on record: +150 g average, 231 g spread and only 57% within ±150 g.

2. Where the variation comes from

A variance-components model splits each reading's error into three parts: day-to-day drift, differences between filling heads, and random cylinder-to-cylinder scatter.

PeriodDay-to-day driftHead-to-headRandom scatter
20240 g (0%)15 g (10%)47 g (90%)
202638 g (10%)32 g (7%)108 g (83%)
All34 g (10%)26 g (6%)98 g (84%)

Recording tare to 0.1 kg alone adds about 29 g of scatter. Removing it, the true machine scatter rose from about 37 g in 2024 to about 104 g in 2026, roughly 2.8 times. Head differences are real: the head effect is statistically significant (F = 3.85 on 23 and 908 degrees of freedom).

3. How the machines fill

If a machine filled to one fixed gross weight, heavier cylinders would come out short, by about 1 g for each 1 g of extra tare. If it simply added 12 kg on top of any leftover gas, the leftover would pass straight through as overfill. Neither happens:

  • Tare effect: −0.02 g per g (95% CI −0.05 to +0.003), so tare is correctly taken into account.
  • Leftover-gas effect: +0.07 g per g (95% CI −0.04 to +0.19), so leftover gas is compensated.
  • Together, tare and leftover gas explain only 1% of the error.

The machines aim at the right net content. The errors come from the set-point drifting and from scatter in the fill valves and load cells, so recalibration and maintenance are the remedy, not a change to how tare is handled.

4. Filling heads

17 of the 24 heads overfill on average with 95% confidence; none underfill. Five heads are both significant and more than 60 g over.

6. LPG over-delivered, and the link to tank stock

Each day's bias was interpolated between check days and multiplied by that day's 12 kg cylinder production from the daily reports. The total is 42.5 MT (95% CI 11.8–73.2 MT) over 1 March – 25 October 2026, a production-weighted average of +20.8 g per cylinder. This is lower than the +47 g average of the checks because July, the busiest month, ran slightly under.

For review by a qualified engineer. Over-filling means more gas leaves the tanks than the books record (cylinders × 12 kg). On its own it should make measured stock fall about 0.21 MT per day below the book figure on the 187 measured days. The operations report instead found measured stock about 1.06 MT per day above the book figure. So the tank-measurement offset is closer to +1.27 MT per day, which strengthens the case for measured density and temperature correction.

7. Machine-learning models

What drives each cylinder's error (neural network). A neural network and a gradient-boosting model were trained on 972 checks and tested on whole check rounds they had not seen. Cross-validated R² is only 0.02 for the neural network and 0.10 for gradient boosting: an individual cylinder's error is mostly unpredictable scatter. Of the part that can be explained, the filling head carries 40% of the weight, the check date (drift over time) 31%, the year 18%, leftover gas 7% and tare 4%.

Unusual check rounds (autoencoder and Isolation Forest). Both models flagged the same four of 41 rounds:

  • 3 Jul 2026: the largest underfill of any round, −43 g average, with 120 g spread.
  • 10 Jan 2026: +150 g average and 231 g spread.
  • 14 Feb and 10 Feb 2026: spreads of 211 g and 209 g.

At cylinder level, the most unusual reading is head 2 on 2 Mar 2026. That cylinder came back with 1.40 kg of gas still in it and was filled 460 g short. In total, 40 cylinders came back with more than 200 g of leftover gas.

Assumptions

  1. Net content = weight after filling − tare; target 12 kg (20, 35 or 45 kg for those sizes).
  2. Tolerance ±150 g for 12 kg (±100 g also shown), and a capability target of Cpk 1.33; both are benchmarks, not plant targets.
  3. Filling head = the machine number; the row number is used where the machine number is blank.
  4. The sheet name is the date. The AGM REPORT and "For Amzad vai" sheets are demo sheets and are left out of every figure.
  5. Valid rows: non-zero weights, weight before filling not more than 0.3 kg below tare, and net error within ±1 kg.
  6. Over-delivery: each day's bias is interpolated linearly between 2026 check days and held flat outside them, then multiplied by that day's 12 kg production. It assumes each check round represents that day's whole production.
  7. Next-check forecast assumes a check about one week after 25 October.

Methods: X-bar and S control charts (n = 24, limits from 2024); Cp and Cpk with bootstrap intervals; a two-way variance-components model; least-squares regression of error on tare and leftover gas; per-head bootstrap intervals (4,000 resamples); binary-segmentation change points; a neural network and gradient boosting with round-grouped cross-validation and permutation importance; an autoencoder (8→6→2→6→8) and Isolation Forest; and a rolling one-step-ahead backtest of 7 forecast methods.
